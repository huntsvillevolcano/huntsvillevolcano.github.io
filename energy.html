02 / ENERGY & HEAT

Earth's heat.
On tap?

Geothermal energy already heats buildings and generates electricity around the world. Could extreme-heat research help expand what is possible?

THE ENERGY CASE

Geothermal's strength is dependable, around-the-clock heat — but drilling, geology and upfront capital matter.

Geothermal is already real

Geothermal systems use heat from the Earth. Depending on the resource, they can provide direct heat for buildings and industry or generate electricity by using underground steam or hot water to drive turbines. Enhanced geothermal systems seek to access heat in hot rock by engineering underground reservoirs, though technical and economic challenges remain.

Iceland is a prominent example of direct-use geothermal energy: the country's National Energy Authority reports that about 90% of domestic space heating came from geothermal energy in 2020. Countries including Kenya, Indonesia, New Zealand, the Philippines and the United States also use geothermal resources for electricity, with deployment shaped by local geology.

What does geothermal electricity cost?

Cost comparisons depend on the resource, financing, drilling risk, plant design, capacity factor, transmission and whether storage or backup is included. One widely used metric is levelized cost of electricity (LCOE), an estimated lifetime average cost per unit of electricity. It is useful for comparison, but it is not the same as a retail electricity bill or a guarantee of a project's actual cost.

USD, global weighted-average LCOE for newly commissioned projects in 2024. These are global benchmarks, not estimates for Huntsville or for a hypothetical constructed volcano.

IRENA's 2024 analysis also reported an 88% global weighted-average capacity factor for newly commissioned geothermal plants — a measure of how much output a plant delivers relative to running at full capacity all year. That is a key advantage: geothermal can provide firm power, unlike weather-dependent generation, but its upfront drilling and development costs can be high.

How does it compare with conventional energy?

For a U.S. market-oriented comparison, Lazard's 2024 analysis estimated unsubsidized LCOE ranges of roughly $64–$106/MWh for geothermal, $29–$92/MWh for utility-scale solar and $27–$73/MWh for onshore wind. Its estimates for combined-cycle gas were approximately $45–$108/MWh and for coal approximately $69–$168/MWh. These ranges overlap, and project-specific conditions can change the ranking substantially.

These comparisons do not prove that a Huntsville volcano could produce economical power. A constructed high-temperature system would have unknown capital costs, operating risks, thermal efficiency and resource life. The research question is whether any technology developed for the project could make geothermal heat extraction safer, more efficient or less expensive.

Potential Huntsville applications

  • Research into high-temperature heat exchangers and closed-loop geothermal systems.
  • Direct heat for industrial processes, greenhouses or district heating if a viable resource and customer base exist.
  • Electricity generation using appropriate steam or binary-cycle technology.
  • Thermal storage and integration with solar, wind and grid systems.
Bottom line: Geothermal is a proven energy technology in suitable locations. A constructed volcano is not a proven or currently feasible geothermal resource. It would need to demonstrate a net-positive energy balance and competitive lifecycle economics.
